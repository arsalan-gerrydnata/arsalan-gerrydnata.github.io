Import Duty and Taxes: How the Calculation Is Built

The structure behind a customs assessment, explained with an illustrative example.

Last updated: 11 November 2026 · General information for planning; procedures and fees can change, so confirm with your clearing agent and operator.

Importers often confuse two bills: the warehouse operator's charges, which our calculators estimate, and the customs duties and taxes, which are decided by the tariff for your goods. This article explains the structure of the second bill, so you can budget correctly. The rates below are illustrative only. Your actual rates depend on the classification of your goods and current law, so always confirm with your clearing agent.

Step 1: the assessable value

Duty is usually calculated on the customs value of the goods, commonly the invoice price plus freight and insurance up to the point of entry. If your invoice shows the goods at one amount and freight separately, both normally form part of the value for assessment. The value is converted into rupees at the exchange rate used by customs.

Step 2: the classification

Every product is classified under a tariff code (HS code). The code determines the customs duty rate and sometimes additional duties, regulatory requirements and whether an exemption applies. Wrong classification is one of the most common causes of customs queries.

Step 3: layering of duties and taxes

Several levies are typically applied one after another. The exact list and rates vary, but a common structure includes customs duty on the assessable value, a further duty or regulatory duty on some goods, sales tax on the value plus the duties, and an advance income tax on the importer. Because later taxes are calculated on top of earlier ones, the total effect is larger than the simple sum of percentages.

An illustrative example

These percentages are made up to show the mechanism; they are not current rates for any product.

On top of this, you pay the warehouse operator's bill. For 100 kg of general cargo cleared on day 4 that is Rs. 22,523 in our calculator, a small share of the total compared with duties in this example, but one you control through speed.

What importers can control

  • Check the classification with your agent before you buy.
  • Ask whether any exemption or concessionary rate applies to your goods.
  • Make sure the declared value reflects the true transaction value.
  • Keep all certificates and invoices ready for assessment.

This article is general information and not tax or legal advice. Rates and rules change, so confirm with a qualified customs agent or tax adviser.

Why assessments get revised

  • The declared value is much lower than comparable imports.
  • The product description does not match its classification code.
  • Missing certificates, such as origin or conformity documents.
  • Differences between invoice, packing list and airway bill.

Questions to ask your agent before you order

  1. Which classification code applies to my product, and why?
  2. Are there regulatory requirements before import?
  3. Do any exemptions or concessionary rates apply?
  4. What total duty and taxes should I budget for this value?
  5. How long does assessment usually take for this type of goods?

Frequently asked questions

Where can I find the duty rate for my product?

Your clearing agent can look it up by HS code in the customs tariff. Always confirm the current rate.

Does freight cost add to duty?

Usually yes. Freight and insurance are normally included in the assessable value.

Can duty be paid after the cargo arrives?

Duties are paid after assessment and before the goods are released.

What if customs disagrees with my invoice value?

Customs can ask for evidence and may re-assess the value. Keep supplier documents and payment proof.

Does this site calculate duties?

No. It only estimates the warehouse operator's charges.
